Supplement Expiry Dates: What They Actually Mean

A date printed on a supplement bottle is easy to misread. It is not a safety deadline in the way a food use-by date is, and it is not a guarantee that the product stays at full strength until that day. Most supplement dates are 'best before' or 'best by' dates: the manufacturer's estimate of how long the product keeps its stated potency when stored as directed.

Potency fades gradually, not overnight. Vitamins, minerals and botanical extracts can lose strength through heat, light, moisture and air, so a bottle kept in a steamy bathroom may age faster than one kept cool and dry. That is why storage instructions matter as much as the printed date: a tight lid, a cool cupboard and keeping the bottle away from the cooker or a sunny windowsill all slow the decline.

Three checks help you judge a bottle sensibly. First, read the date itself and note whether it says 'best before', 'expires' or 'manufactured' — a manufacture date alone tells you nothing about shelf life without the stated period. Second, look for signs of damage: a broken seal, clumping, an odd smell or discolouration are reasons to stop, whatever the date says. Third, check the label for storage directions and serving information, and compare them with independent guidance on reading labels — The Supplement Rating publishes a practical label-checking guide that walks through exactly these checks.

If a product is well past its best-before date, the honest question is not whether it will harm you — most dry supplements simply weaken — but whether you are still getting what the label promises. When in doubt, replace it rather than guessing at the dose.
